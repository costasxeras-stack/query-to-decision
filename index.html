What drives bad reviews on a marketplace, and what should it fix first?

Olist connects small Brazilian shops to large marketplaces. One in seven of its reviews is 1 or 2 stars. Every query below runs live in your browser on the real data. Edit any of them and run it again.

The answer in 30 seconds

  1. Delivery decides the rating. Late orders get a bad review 62% of the time, on-time orders 9%.
  2. Speed is not the problem. Orders arrive 12 days before the promised date on average, and arriving 3 weeks early earns no better rating than 1 week early.
  3. Broken promises cluster by place, not by bad sellers. Rio de Janeiro has 13% of orders but 23% of late ones.
  4. Fix reliability on the worst routes first. Better delivery estimates help, but only a little.
S

Would smarter promises fix it?

If estimates are padded, why not promise each route's real delivery time? I fitted route-based promises (seller state to customer state) on orders from 2016 and 2017, and tested them on orders from 2018. This step runs in Python, because percentiles are awkward in SQLite. See the script.

FindingThere is no free lunch. Cutting today's promises by 2 days makes 11.3% of orders late. A route-based promise of the same average length as today (23.2 against 23.4 days) is late 7.3% of the time against 7.7%. Better estimates are worth doing because they are cheap, but they are not the main lever.

Recommendation

For a marketplace product team, in order of impact.

1. Make the worst routes reliable, starting with Rio de Janeiro

Rio and the north-east states have the highest late rates, and Rio alone holds 23% of late orders. Work with carriers and sellers on those routes: carrier choice, pickup cut-off times, and a stock-out check before the order is accepted.

Measure: late rate on Rio routes (12.1% today, target the national 6.8%). Guardrail: average promised days must not rise.

2. Tell customers before they find out

Orders that never arrive are 3% of orders but 15% of bad reviews. Send a proactive message and a fix when a parcel misses a tracking milestone, instead of waiting for the customer to complain.

Measure: share of 1 and 2 star reviews on late and undelivered orders (62% and 78% today).

3. Switch to route-based delivery estimates

A cheap change with a small gain: fewer late orders at the same promise length. Do not shorten promises across the board: the simulation shows lateness rises fast.

Measure: late rate at equal average promise. Hypothesis to test: shorter, more accurate promises raise checkout conversion. This data has no checkout events, so it needs an A/B test.

4. Say when an order ships in several parcels

Orders from two or more sellers are only 1.3% of orders, but 47% of them get a bad review. Showing "arrives in 2 parcels" at checkout is a small, testable change.

Measure: bad review rate on multi-seller orders.

Size of the prizeIf every late order had been rated like an on-time one, bad reviews would fall from 14,486 to about 11,095 (−23%) and the average rating from 4.09 to 4.22. This is an upper bound from step 10, not a forecast.

Limits

  • Data from 2016 to 2018. Olist's logistics have surely changed since.
  • Dates are kept to the day. "Late" means delivered on a later day than the promised date.
  • Ratings and lateness are correlated here. Late delivery very likely causes bad reviews, but this data cannot prove it on its own. The upper bound assumes it does.
  • No checkout or browsing data, so the effect of promise length on conversion is a hypothesis.
  • IDs are shortened to numbers, and review text and payments are left out to keep the database small. Nothing else is changed.
